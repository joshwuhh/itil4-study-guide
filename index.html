LO 1 — Key Concepts BL1+BL2 ~5 marks

Covers LO 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 — recall definitions AND describe key concepts of value, relationships, and service management.

1.1 Core Definitions (Bloom's Level 1 — must recall exactly)

Definition — Service Management

A set of specialized organizational capabilities for enabling value for customers in the form of services.

Definition — Service

A means of enabling value co-creation by facilitating outcomes that customers want to achieve, without the customer having to manage specific costs and risks.

Definition — Value

The perceived benefits, usefulness, and importance of something. Value is subjective — it is defined by the stakeholder's perception.

Definition — Utility

The functionality offered by a product or service to meet a particular need. Utility = "what the service does" = fit for purpose. A service must either support consumer performance OR remove constraints (or both).

Definition — Warranty

Assurance that a product or service will meet agreed requirements. Warranty = "how the service performs" = fit for use. Relates to availability, capacity, security, and continuity.

Key memory trick: Utility = fit for Purpose (what it Provides). Warranty = fit for Use (how it Uptime/availability performs). Both are required for value.
Definition — Customer

A person who defines the requirements for a service and takes responsibility for the outcomes of service consumption.

Definition — User

A person who uses services.

Definition — Sponsor

A person who authorizes budget for service consumption.

Definition — Organization

A person or a group of people that has its own functions with responsibilities, authorities, and relationships to achieve its objectives.

Common trap: One person can hold all three roles simultaneously (e.g. an individual buying a personal phone plan is user + customer + sponsor). But in a corporate setting, these are often separate people.

1.2 Creating Value — Key Concepts

TermDefinition
OutputA tangible or intangible deliverable of an activity (e.g. a report, a car)
OutcomeA result for a stakeholder enabled by one or more outputs (e.g. preserved memories, mobility)
CostThe amount of money spent on a specific activity or resource. Two types: costs removed from consumer (value) and costs imposed on consumer
RiskA possible event that could cause harm or loss. Two types: risks removed from consumer (value) and risks imposed on consumer
Value co-creationValue is created through active collaboration between provider and consumer — not delivered to a passive consumer

1.3 Service Relationships

Definition — Service Offering

A formal description of one or more services, designed to address the needs of a target consumer group. May include: goods (ownership transferred), access to resources (no ownership transfer), service actions (performed by provider).

TermWhat it means
Service relationshipCooperation between provider and consumer including provision, consumption, and relationship management
Service provisionActivities performed by an org to provide services (managing resources, ensuring access, fulfilling service actions)
Service consumptionActivities performed by an org to consume services
Service relationship managementJoint activities by provider AND consumer to ensure continual value co-creation

LO 2 — Guiding Principles BL2 ~6 marks

Explain all 7 principles and how they interact. Bloom's Level 2 — you need to understand and apply them, not just name them.

What is a Guiding Principle?

A recommendation that guides an organization in all circumstances, regardless of changes in goals, strategies, work type, or management structure. They are universal and enduring.

Mnemonic: F-S-P-C-T-K-O — Focus · Start · Progress · Collaborate · Think · Keep · Optimize
#PrincipleCore ideaKey phrase
1 Focus on value Everything must map directly or indirectly to value for stakeholders. Know who the service consumer is and what they value. "Why does this matter to the customer?"
2 Start where you are Don't start from scratch. Observe the current state directly. Leverage what exists. Measure, don't just report. "What can we reuse?"
3 Progress iteratively with feedback Organize work into smaller manageable sections. Use feedback before, during, and after each iteration. Avoid analysis paralysis. "Work in small loops; learn and adjust."
4 Collaborate and promote visibility Include the right people. Make work visible. Avoid silos. Share information widely. Collaboration ≠ consensus. "Silos kill services."
5 Think and work holistically No service or element stands alone. Manage end-to-end. Consider all four dimensions. Integration matters. "The whole is more than its parts."
6 Keep it simple and practical Use minimum steps to achieve the objective. If it doesn't create value, eliminate it. Don't over-engineer for exceptions. "If in doubt, leave it out."
7 Optimize and automate Use human resources for complex decisions; automate repetitive tasks. Optimize FIRST, then automate. Technology ≠ solution on its own. "Simplify before automating."

Principle Interaction (LO 2.1)

Principles are not used in isolation — they interact and reinforce each other. Example: Progress iteratively works with Think holistically to ensure each iteration includes all necessary elements. Organizations should consider the relevance of all 7 in every situation.

Exam approach: Scenario questions will describe a situation and ask which principle is being applied. Look for: value → #1; existing assets → #2; small iterations → #3; transparency/teams → #4; end-to-end → #5; eliminating steps → #6; automation → #7.

LO 3 — Four Dimensions BL2 ~2 marks

Describe all four dimensions of service management. They apply to every service and to the SVS as a whole.

Key message

Four dimensions that are collectively critical to effective and efficient facilitation of value. Failing to address all four leads to undeliverable services or services that don't meet quality/efficiency expectations.

DimensionCoversKey point
1. Organizations & People Formal structures, culture, roles, responsibilities, competencies, management styles Culture that supports objectives. Right skills at right levels. Leaders must champion shared values.
2. Information & Technology Information needed to manage services, technologies required, knowledge bases, workflow systems, AI, cloud What information is managed? How is it protected? Is technology compatible, viable, and aligned with strategy?
3. Partners & Suppliers Relationships with other organizations involved in service design, development, deployment, support Ranges from formal contracts to flexible partnerships. Service integration and management (SIAM) may be used.
4. Value Streams & Processes Activities, workflows, controls, procedures needed to achieve objectives. Value streams + processes. A value stream = series of steps to create and deliver products/services. A process = set of activities transforming inputs to outputs.
External factors (PESTLE: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental) constrain and influence all four dimensions but are outside the organization's control.

LO 4 — ITIL Service Value System BL2 ~1 mark

Describe the purpose and components of the SVS. Know what goes in and what comes out.

Purpose

The SVS describes how all the components and activities of the organization work together as a system to enable value creation.

ElementDescription
InputsOpportunity + Demand (from internal and external sources)
OutputsValue (for organization, customers, other stakeholders)
Guiding principlesRecommendations that guide the organization in all circumstances
GovernanceMeans by which the organization is directed and controlled (Evaluate, Direct, Monitor)
Service value chainSet of interconnected activities to deliver valuable products/services
PracticesSets of organizational resources for performing work or accomplishing objectives
Continual improvementRecurring activity at all levels ensuring performance meets stakeholder expectations
Big idea: The SVS specifically discourages siloed working. Components can be combined in multiple value streams flexibly. This is what supports Agile, DevOps, and Lean alongside traditional approaches.

LO 5 — Service Value Chain BL2 ~2 marks

Describe the 6 activities and their purposes. Know how they interconnect. Know the fixed rules.

Fixed Rules — Always True

• All incoming/outgoing interactions with external parties → via Engage
• All new resources obtained → via Obtain/Build
• Planning at all levels → via Plan
• Improvements initiated/managed → via Improve

ActivityPurposeKey outputs
Plan Ensure a shared understanding of the vision, current status, and improvement direction for all four dimensions and all products/services Strategic/tactical/operational plans, portfolio decisions, architectures, policies
Improve Ensure continual improvement of products, services, and practices across all value chain activities and four dimensions Improvement initiatives, value chain performance information, improvement status reports
Engage Provide understanding of stakeholder needs, transparency, continual engagement, and good relationships Consolidated demands, product requirements, user support tasks, contracts/agreements
Design & Transition Ensure products and services continually meet stakeholder expectations for quality, costs, and time to market Requirements and specs for obtain/build, new/changed products for deliver and support
Obtain/Build Ensure service components are available when and where needed and meet agreed specifications Service components for deliver and support; knowledge about new/changed service components
Deliver & Support Ensure services are delivered and supported according to agreed specifications and stakeholder expectations Services delivered to customers/users; product and service performance information
Value stream = a specific combination of value chain activities designed for a particular scenario. Each activity converts inputs into outputs using ITIL practices.

LO 7a — Continual Improvement BL2 Deep part of 17 marks

Explain the practice AND the 7-step Continual Improvement Model. This is one of the highest-weighted areas.

Purpose
Align the organization's practices and services with changing business needs through the ongoing improvement of products, services, and practices, or any element involved in management of products and services.
Continual Improvement Register (CIR)

A database or structured document to track and manage improvement ideas from identification through to final action. Multiple CIRs can exist at individual, team, departmental, or organizational levels.

The 7-Step Continual Improvement Model

Flow: Linked to the organization's goals. Steps do not have to be strictly linear — you may return to a previous step.
1

What is the vision?

Define the vision of the initiative. Translate the organization's vision and objectives for the specific business unit/team. Provides context for all subsequent decisions.

2

Where are we now?

Current state assessment. Objective measurement of existing services, people's competencies, processes, culture. Establishes the baseline (Point A). Without this, you can't measure improvement.

3

Where do we want to be?

Gap analysis. Define the target state (Point B). Set prioritized improvement objectives, CSFs, and KPIs using SMART criteria (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound). Target state = progress toward vision, not the whole vision.

4

How do we get there?

Create a specific plan. May involve experiments. Best approach may not be immediately clear. Work may be iterative — each iteration moves the improvement forward.

5

Take action

Execute the plan. Can be waterfall or Agile. Remain open to changing direction. Continually measure progress toward the vision. Results in a new current state.

6

Did we get there?

Validate success. Check progress (did we achieve objectives?) AND value (are those objectives still relevant?). If not achieved, take additional action — new iteration. Don't assume success without checking.

7

How do we keep the momentum going?

Market the successes. Reinforce new methods using organizational change management and knowledge management. Embed changes so they don't revert. If failed, communicate lessons learned transparently.

LO 7b — Change Enablement BL2 Deep

Formerly called "Change Control" in the syllabus. Know the 3 types of change and the change authority concept.

Purpose
Maximize the number of successful service and product changes by ensuring risks have been properly assessed, authorizing changes to proceed, and managing the change schedule.
Definition — Change

The addition, modification, or removal of anything that could have a direct or indirect effect on services.

Three Types of Change

TypeDescriptionAuthorizationExamples
Standard Low-risk, pre-authorized, well-understood, fully documented. Risk assessed when procedure is first created/modified — not each time. Pre-authorized — no additional authorization needed each time Password reset, software patch installation
Normal Must be scheduled, assessed, and authorized following a process. Can be low risk (rapid approval) to very major (board-level approval). Change authority (varies by risk level) New application release, infrastructure upgrade
Emergency Must be implemented as soon as possible (incident resolution, security patch). Expedited assessment/authorization. Documentation may be deferred. Small group of senior managers; separate emergency change authority Security vulnerability patch, critical system fix
Change Authority

The person or group who authorizes a change. Correct change authority must be assigned to each change type for efficiency and effectiveness.

Change Schedule

Used to plan changes, assist in communication, avoid conflicts, and assign resources. Also used after deployment for incident management, problem management, and improvement planning.

Don't confuse: Change control ≠ Organizational change management. Organizational change management handles the people aspects. Change control handles product and service changes.

LO 7c — Incident Management BL2 Deep

Purpose
Minimize the negative impact of incidents by restoring normal service operation as quickly as possible.
Definition — Incident

An unplanned interruption to a service or reduction in the quality of a service.

Key Concepts

  • Every incident should be logged and managed
  • Incidents are prioritized based on business impact — highest impact resolved first
  • Target resolution times are agreed, documented, and communicated
  • Separate processes for major incidents and information security incidents
  • Incident records should link to related CIs, changes, problems, known errors

Resolution Paths

  • Users self-help
  • Service desk resolves
  • Escalated to support team (based on category)
  • Escalated to suppliers or partners
  • Temporary team assembled for major incidents (swarming)
  • Disaster recovery plans invoked (extreme cases)
Swarming

A technique where many different stakeholders work together initially on an incident, until it becomes clear which is best placed to continue. Others move on to other tasks.

Incidents vs Problems: Incidents have immediate user impact → restore service fast. Problems are the causes → investigate and prevent future incidents. They run in parallel but are distinct.

LO 7d — Problem Management BL2 Deep

Purpose
Reduce the likelihood and impact of incidents by identifying actual and potential causes of incidents, and managing workarounds and known errors.
TermDefinition
ProblemA cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents
Known errorA problem that has been analysed but has not been resolved
WorkaroundA solution that reduces or eliminates the impact of an incident/problem for which a full resolution is not yet available

Three Phases of Problem Management

PhaseWhat happens
1. Problem identification Identify and log problems. Sources: trend analysis of incidents, duplicate/recurring issues, major incident review, supplier input, dev/test teams
2. Problem control Prioritize problems by risk (impact + probability). Analyse and document workarounds. Document as known errors. Not all problems need full analysis.
3. Error control Manage known errors. Find permanent solutions — may result in a change request. Re-assess known errors regularly. Evaluate workaround effectiveness.
Key distinction: Problem management identifies and analyses. Implementing the resolution (change) is typically outside problem management scope — that goes to change enablement.

LO 7e — Service Request Management BL2 Deep

Purpose
Support the agreed quality of a service by handling all pre-defined, user-initiated service requests in an effective and user-friendly manner.
Definition — Service Request

A request from a user (or authorized representative) that initiates a service action which has been agreed as a normal part of service delivery.

Types of Service Request

  • Request for a service delivery action (e.g. provide a report)
  • Request for information (e.g. "what are the office hours?")
  • Request for provision of a resource (e.g. new phone, virtual server)
  • Request for access to a resource (e.g. file/folder access)
  • Feedback, compliments, and complaints
Critical distinction: Service requests are NOT incidents. They are normal, expected, pre-defined parts of service delivery. Incidents are unplanned failures. Service requests use standard changes.

Key Guidelines

  • Standardize and automate to greatest degree possible
  • Set clear policies on what requires approval vs. what is auto-fulfilled
  • Clearly set expectations for fulfilment times
  • Some requests can be fully automated (software install, virtual server provisioning)

LO 7f — Service Desk BL2 Deep

Purpose
Capture demand for incident resolution and service requests. The entry point and single point of contact for the service provider with all of its users.

Key Characteristics

  • Provides a clear path for users to report issues, queries, requests
  • Focus is on people and business, not just technical issues
  • Must have deep understanding of the business context and user experience
  • Staff need: empathy, incident prioritization, effective communication, emotional intelligence
  • Has a major influence on user experience and how the service provider is perceived

Access Channels

  • Phone (including IVR, voice recognition)
  • Service portals and mobile applications
  • Chat (live and chatbots)
  • Email
  • Walk-in service desks
  • Text and social media

Models

  • Centralized — single location, supports consistent coverage
  • Virtual — agents work from multiple locations; cloud-based; more complex routing
  • Automated / bot-based — self-service via portals; AI-driven
Trend: Service desks are moving toward more self-service via AI, RPA, and chatbots — reducing phone contact, enabling focus on excellent CX when personal contact is needed.

LO 7g — Service Level Management BL2 Deep

Purpose
Set clear business-based targets for service levels, and ensure that delivery of services is properly assessed, monitored, and managed against these targets.

What SLM Does

  • Establishes a shared view of services and target levels with customers
  • Ensures the org meets defined service levels (collection, analysis, storage, reporting)
  • Performs service reviews — does current service still meet needs?
  • Captures and reports on service issues and performance
Definition — Service Level Agreement (SLA)

A documented agreement between a service provider and a customer that identifies both services required and the expected level of service.

What Makes a Good SLA?

  • Related to a defined service in the service catalogue
  • Relates to outcomes, not just operational metrics
  • Represents genuine agreement (engagement with stakeholders)
  • Simply written and easy to understand
The Watermelon SLA Effect

When an SLA appears green on the outside (provider thinks it's meeting targets) but is actually red inside (customer is dissatisfied). Happens when metrics don't reflect actual customer experience. Example: 99.9% uptime SLA met, but the 0.1% downtime was during the customer's critical business transaction.

SLM Approach: Engage and Listen

  • Customer engagement — understand actual ongoing needs, not just original agreements
  • Customer feedback — surveys, business metrics, operational metrics, business measures

Quick Reference — All Key Definitions Flashcard Ready

Tap any card to reveal a real-world example. Cover the definition and test yourself.

Service Management
A set of specialized organizational capabilities for enabling value for customers in the form of services.
Real World
An IT department that runs the company's email, laptop provisioning, and helpdesk. Their ability to consistently keep email running, get new hires set up quickly, and resolve issues is their "service management" — the collective skill and organization behind making those services work reliably.
Service
A means of enabling value co-creation by facilitating outcomes that customers want to achieve, without the customer having to manage specific costs and risks.
Real World
Uber is a service. You want to get from A to B (outcome) without owning a car, hiring a driver, managing insurance, or dealing with maintenance (costs and risks managed by Uber). You co-create value — you tell Uber where you're going, they handle everything else.
Value
The perceived benefits, usefulness, and importance of something. Value is subjective — defined by the stakeholder's perception.
Real World
A 99.9% uptime SLA might be highly valued by an e-commerce company (every minute of downtime = lost revenue) but barely matter to an internal HR system used only 9–5. Same service, different perceived value. This is why SLAs must be negotiated per customer, not set uniformly.
Organization
A person or group of people that has its own functions with responsibilities, authorities, and relationships to achieve its objectives.
Real World
A single freelance IT consultant is an organization. So is a 50,000-person global bank. Both have defined responsibilities and objectives. In ITIL, the same entity can be both a service provider (to its customers) and a service consumer (buying cloud services from AWS).
Customer
A person who defines the requirements for a service and takes responsibility for the outcomes of service consumption.
Real World
The Head of Sales at a retail company who tells IT "we need a CRM that handles 10,000 contacts, integrates with email, and is available 24/7." They define the requirements and are accountable if sales targets aren't met because the CRM fails. They are the customer — even if they never personally log into the CRM.
User
A person who uses services.
Real World
The sales reps who log into the CRM every day to manage their pipeline. They didn't define the requirements (that was the Head of Sales / customer) and didn't approve the budget (that was the CFO / sponsor) — but they use the system daily. Their experience directly shapes service desk ticket volume.
Sponsor
A person who authorizes the budget for service consumption.
Real World
The CFO who signs off the $50,000/year Salesforce contract. They may never use the CRM and didn't specify what it needs to do — but without their budget approval, the service doesn't get purchased. In a small business, the owner might be customer + user + sponsor all at once.
Utility
The functionality offered by a product or service to meet a particular need. "Fit for purpose." What the service DOES.
Real World
A store's Wi-Fi has utility if it lets staff process card payments and access the inventory system. If it can't handle those tasks — even if it's technically "on" — it lacks utility. Your store's POS system has utility: it processes transactions, prints receipts, tracks inventory. That's the "what it does."
Warranty
Assurance that a product or service will meet agreed requirements. "Fit for use." How the service PERFORMS.
Real World
Your store's POS system has warranty if it's available during all trading hours, processes transactions in under 3 seconds, and never loses a sale due to downtime. The system might have all the right features (utility) but if it crashes every Saturday afternoon — your busiest time — it fails on warranty. Both are needed for value.
Output
A tangible or intangible deliverable of an activity.
Real World
A weekly sales report generated by the IT team is an output — it's the tangible thing produced. A trained employee after completing onboarding is also an output (intangible). The report itself isn't the point — what the manager does with it (makes decisions, spots trends) is the outcome.
Outcome
A result for a stakeholder enabled by one or more outputs.
Real World
The weekly sales report (output) enables the store manager to identify that Tuesday afternoons are dead and Friday evenings are understaffed — then reschedule accordingly. The outcome is better-staffed shifts and higher sales. IT provided the output; the business achieved the outcome. ITIL says providers should care about outcomes, not just outputs.
Cost
The amount of money spent on a specific activity or resource. Services both remove costs from consumers and impose new ones.
Real World
Using a cloud payroll service (e.g. ADP) removes costs: you don't need a payroll accountant, payroll software, or compliance expertise. But it imposes costs: monthly subscription fee, time to integrate with your HR system. A service that removes more cost than it imposes creates value — if it doesn't, it's not worth having.
Risk
A possible event that could cause harm or loss, or make it harder to achieve objectives. Services both remove risks and impose new ones.
Real World
Using AWS for hosting removes risks: you don't manage physical servers, power, cooling, or hardware failure. But it imposes new risks: vendor lock-in, data sovereignty issues, internet dependency. A good service provider makes this trade-off clearly visible so consumers can make informed decisions.
Service Offering
A formal description of one or more services designed to address the needs of a target consumer group. May include goods, resource access, and service actions.
Real World
An IT department's "New Employee Setup" service offering includes: a laptop (good — ownership transferred), access to email/Slack/systems (resource access — no ownership), and IT orientation session (service action — performed by IT). Together, these address the new hire's need to be productive from day one.
Service Relationship
A cooperation between a service provider and consumer, including provision, consumption, and relationship management.
Real World
Your store's relationship with its EPOS (till) software vendor. The vendor provides the system and support (provision). Your staff use it daily (consumption). You have quarterly review calls, raise enhancement requests, and escalate issues (relationship management). All three together = the service relationship.
Service Provision
Activities performed by an organization to provide services — managing resources, ensuring access, fulfilling service actions.
Real World
Everything your mobile network (e.g. Verizon) does to give you phone service: maintaining cell towers, managing spectrum, routing calls, running billing systems, staffing customer support. You never see most of it — but it's all service provision happening behind the scenes.
Service Consumption
Activities performed by an organization to consume services — using resources, receiving service actions, enabling outcomes.
Real World
Your store consuming Microsoft 365: paying the subscription, setting up user accounts, training staff, managing licenses, contacting support when something breaks. The store is actively doing things to consume the service — it's not passive. Poor consumption (e.g. not training staff) leads to poor outcomes even if the service itself is great.
Value Stream
A series of steps an organization undertakes to create and deliver products and services to consumers.
Real World
When a user calls the service desk with a broken laptop: log ticket → diagnose → order replacement → image and configure → deliver to user → close ticket. That end-to-end sequence of steps — crossing IT, procurement, and logistics — is a value stream. Each step adds value toward the outcome: a working laptop.
Process
A set of interrelated or interacting activities that transform inputs into outputs. A defined, repeatable way of doing something.
Real World
The incident management process: receive report → log → categorize → prioritize → investigate → resolve → close → review. Input: reported disruption. Output: resolved incident record and restored service. The process is the same regardless of who runs it or which system went down — that's what makes it a process.
IT Asset
Any financially valuable component that can contribute to the delivery of an IT product or service.
Real World
In your store: the tills (hardware), the POS software license, the Wi-Fi router, the server in the back room, even the software code for a custom app. All are IT assets because they have financial value and contribute to IT service delivery. IT asset management tracks these across their full lifecycle — purchase, use, maintenance, disposal.
Event
Any change of state that has significance for the management of a service or other CI. Detected via monitoring tools.
Real World
Your server's CPU hits 95% utilization — a monitoring tool detects this change of state and logs an event. Not all events are bad: "backup completed successfully" is also an event. Events are classified as: informational (normal), warning (approaching threshold), or exception (something has gone wrong → may become an incident).
Configuration Item (CI)
Any component that needs to be managed in order to deliver an IT service.
Real World
The store's Wi-Fi router is a CI — IT needs to know it exists, what firmware it runs, what it connects to, and who's responsible for it. A CI can be hardware, software, a network, a building, a person, or even a document (like an SLA). CIs are recorded in a Configuration Management Database (CMDB) so you can trace the impact of any change or failure.
Change
The addition, modification, or removal of anything that could have a direct or indirect effect on services.
Real World
Updating the POS software from v3.1 to v3.2 is a change. So is adding a new till, removing an old server, changing a firewall rule, or updating a user's access permissions. Changes need to be controlled because even a small tweak can break something else — most IT outages are caused by poorly managed changes.
Incident
An unplanned interruption to a service or reduction in the quality of a service.
Real World
The store's card payment system goes down at 12pm on a Saturday. That's an incident — unplanned, impacting service quality. Even a slowdown counts: if the POS normally processes in 2 seconds but is now taking 30 seconds per transaction, that's also an incident (reduction in quality). Goal: restore normal service as fast as possible, not necessarily fix the root cause right now.
Problem
A cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents.
Real World
The card payment system crashes every Saturday. After the third time, IT raises a problem record to investigate the underlying cause. They discover a memory leak in the POS software that builds up over a week and causes crashes. That memory leak is the problem. Fixing the incident (restarting the system) didn't fix the problem — the crash keeps recurring.
Known Error
A problem that has been analysed but has not been resolved.
Real World
IT has confirmed the memory leak in the POS software and identified which module causes it — but a patch from the vendor won't be available for 3 weeks. It's now a known error: diagnosed, documented, not yet fixed. The team documents a workaround (restart the service every Friday night) so staff know what to do when it crashes, without waiting for IT to investigate from scratch each time.
Workaround
A solution that reduces or eliminates the impact of an incident or problem for which a full resolution is not yet available.
Real World
Restarting the POS service every Friday night before the Saturday rush prevents the crash — that's the workaround. It doesn't fix the memory leak (the problem), but it reduces impact to near-zero while waiting for the vendor patch. Some workarounds become permanent if the fix is too costly or complex — the workaround just gets built into standard operating procedure.
Release
A version of a service or other CI, or a collection of CIs, that is made available for use.
Real World
The vendor sends the POS v3.3 patch that fixes the memory leak. IT deploys it to the servers (deployment management) but holds it behind a feature flag — staff can't use it yet. When IT flips the flag and makes v3.3 live to all stores, that's the release. Deployment ≠ release: you can deploy without releasing (staged rollout, testing).
Service Level
One or more metrics that define expected or achieved service quality.
Real World
"The service desk will answer 90% of calls within 20 seconds" and "critical incidents will be resolved within 1 hour" are service levels. They're measurable, specific targets that both sides agree on. Without defined service levels, there's no shared expectation and no way to objectively assess whether the provider is performing.
Service Level Agreement (SLA)
A documented agreement between a service provider and a customer identifying services required and the expected level of service.
Real World
Your store signs an SLA with its EPOS vendor: 99.5% uptime during trading hours, P1 incidents resolved within 2 hours, P2 within 8 hours, monthly performance reports provided. If the vendor misses these, penalties apply. A good SLA reflects business outcomes — not just technical metrics — to avoid the watermelon effect (green metrics, unhappy customer).
Configuration Management System (CMS)
A set of tools, data, and information used to support service configuration management.
Real World
ServiceNow's CMDB module is a CMS. It holds records of every server, laptop, application, and network device — who owns it, what it connects to, what services depend on it. When an incident hits, the responder opens the CMS to understand what else might be affected. Without it, a single router failure could take hours to trace because no one knows what depends on it.

LO 1 — Key Concepts

What is the key difference between utility and warranty?
Utility = "fit for purpose" — what the service does; its functionality to meet a need. A service must support consumer performance or remove constraints.

Warranty = "fit for use" — how the service performs; assurance it meets agreed requirements (availability, capacity, security, continuity).

Both are required for a service to deliver value. A theme park with great rides (utility) that are constantly broken (no warranty) delivers no value.
What are the three roles a service consumer can hold?
Customer — defines requirements and takes responsibility for outcomes
User — uses the service
Sponsor — authorizes the budget

These can be one person (individual phone buyer) or separate people in a corporate setting (CIO = customer, CFO = sponsor, employees = users).
What is the difference between an output and an outcome?
Output = a tangible or intangible deliverable of an activity. (e.g. a wedding photo album)
Outcome = a result for a stakeholder enabled by one or more outputs. (e.g. preserved memories from the album)

Providers deliver outputs; consumers achieve outcomes. Services are about enabling outcomes.
What are the three components that a service offering may include?
Goods — supplied to and owned by the consumer (mobile phone, physical server)
Access to resources — granted/licensed under agreed terms; provider retains ownership (network access, cloud storage)
Service actions — performed by the provider to address consumer needs (user support, equipment replacement)
How does ITIL 4 view value co-creation?
Value is co-created through active collaboration between providers and consumers — not delivered passively from provider to consumer. Providers should not work in isolation to define value. Consumers contribute through defining requirements, clarifying success factors, and participating in service design.

LO 2 — Guiding Principles

What does "Start where you are" mean in practice?
Do not start from scratch without first investigating what already exists. Observe the current state directly (don't just rely on reports). Reuse, repurpose, or build on existing services, processes, people, and tools. Starting over is rarely necessary. Note: measurement should support direct observation, not replace it.
What is analysis paralysis and which guiding principle addresses it?
Analysis paralysis occurs when so much time is spent analyzing a situation that nothing gets done. It is addressed by Progress iteratively with feedback — the principle encourages organizing work into smaller iterations that can be completed quickly, using feedback loops throughout. "Comprehend the whole, but do something."
Does "Collaborate and promote visibility" mean you need everyone's consensus?
No. Collaboration does not mean consensus. Trying to make everyone happy can lead to poor outcomes or inaction. You should include the right people and make work visible, but decisions can and should be made even without full consensus from all stakeholders.
What must happen BEFORE automating according to the guiding principles?
You must optimize first, then automate. Attempting to automate something complex or sub-optimal is unlikely to achieve the desired outcome. Simplify and streamline the process first (using "Keep it simple and practical"), map the standard and repeating steps, then automate. The principle is "Optimize and automate."

LO 3-5 — Dimensions, SVS, Value Chain

What are the four dimensions of service management?
1. Organizations and People — culture, structure, roles, competencies
2. Information and Technology — information, knowledge, technologies
3. Partners and Suppliers — relationships with other organizations
4. Value Streams and Processes — activities, workflows, value streams

They apply to every service and to the SVS as a whole. Failing to address all four leads to service delivery failures.
What are the five components of the ITIL SVS?
1. Guiding principles — universal recommendations
2. Governance — direct and control (Evaluate, Direct, Monitor)
3. Service value chain — six interconnected activities
4. Practices — sets of resources for performing work
5. Continual improvement — recurring activity at all levels

Inputs: Opportunity + Demand. Output: Value.
Which value chain activity handles ALL interactions with external parties?
Engage. All incoming and outgoing interactions with parties external to the value chain are performed via Engage. This is a fixed rule.
What is the purpose of the "Improve" value chain activity?
To ensure continual improvement of products, services, and practices across all value chain activities and the four dimensions of service management. All improvements at all levels are initiated and managed via Improve.

LO 6 — Practice Purposes & Key Terms

What is the difference between a problem and a known error?
Problem = a cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents (not yet analysed)
Known error = a problem that has been analysed but has not been resolved

A problem becomes a known error once initial analysis is complete and the faulty component(s) are identified.
What is the purpose of Release management vs Deployment management?
Release management: Make new and changed services and features available for use (focuses on the "when and what" for users and customers).

Deployment management: Move new or changed hardware, software, documentation, processes to live environments (focuses on the physical act of moving components).

They work closely together but are distinct. A component may be deployed before it is released (e.g. via a feature flag).

LO 7 — Seven Practices in Detail

What are the 7 steps of the Continual Improvement Model?
1. What is the vision?
2. Where are we now? (current state assessment)
3. Where do we want to be? (gap analysis, SMART objectives)
4. How do we get there? (plan)
5. Take action
6. Did we get there? (validate)
7. How do we keep the momentum going?

Steps do not have to be strictly linear.
What are the three types of change in Change Enablement?
Standard — low-risk, pre-authorized, well-understood, no additional authorization needed each time
Normal — must be scheduled, assessed, authorized through a process; change authority varies by risk
Emergency — implemented as fast as possible; expedited assessment/authorization; documentation may be deferred; separate emergency change authority
What is "swarming" in incident management?
A technique where many different stakeholders work together initially on an incident. As it becomes clear which of them is best placed to continue, others move on to other tasks. Used for complex or major incidents requiring collective analysis.
What are the three phases of Problem Management?
1. Problem identification — identify and log problems (trend analysis, duplicate incidents, major incident review, supplier input)
2. Problem control — prioritize by risk, analyse, document workarounds, log as known errors
3. Error control — manage known errors, find permanent solutions (may raise change request), reassess regularly, evaluate workaround effectiveness
What is the "watermelon SLA effect"?
When an SLA appears green on the outside (all metrics technically met) but is actually red inside (customers are dissatisfied). This happens when SLAs are based on individual operational metrics that don't reflect actual customer experience. Example: 99.9% uptime is met, but the 0.1% downtime happened during the customer's most critical business period.
What is the key distinction between a service request and an incident?
Service request — pre-defined, pre-agreed, normal part of service delivery; NOT a failure (e.g. request for new software, access to a folder)
Incident — unplanned interruption or degradation; IS a failure or degradation of service

Service requests typically use standard changes. Incidents require restoration of service as quickly as possible.
What is the purpose of the Service Desk, and what makes it effective?
Purpose: Capture demand for incident resolution and service requests; be the entry point and single point of contact for users.

Effectiveness factors: Empathy, understanding of business context, effective communication, emotional intelligence, ability to prioritize based on business impact. The service desk has a major influence on user experience and how the service provider is perceived.
